# No-Robot Web Copy: Execution Order

The checklist rules, reordered into the sequence you'd actually apply them when writing or editing a page — with the reasoning behind each step. Structure and strategy come first; sentence-level polish comes last. Doing it in reverse is why most edits still sound like a bot with the dashes removed.

---

## 1. Talk to one buyer

**Do this first, before writing anything.** Name the specific person: their role, their situation, what's at stake for them. Not "small business owners" — "a solo founder who just missed a client deadline because invoicing took too long."

*Why first:* every other decision — what problem to lead with, which objection matters, what tone to use — depends on who you think is reading. Skip this and you default to writing for "everyone," which is how copy ends up generic.

## 2. Find the one or two real objections

Before drafting a word of the page, list what's actually stopping *this* buyer from clicking. Not a comprehensive FAQ — the one or two things genuinely in their head ("is this another tool I have to babysit," "will this lock me into a contract").

*Why second:* if you don't know the objection going in, you'll either skip it (page fails to convert) or bolt on a bloated FAQ section at the end (page gets padded). Knowing it early means you can weave it in naturally instead of patching it on.

## 3. Write the above-the-fold section

Headline + subhead that states who this is for and what they get. No warm-up sentence, no mission statement.

*Why third:* this is the highest-leverage real estate on the page. Get it right before anything else, because everything below the fold exists to support this claim — not the other way around.

- **Specific, punchy headline, not padded.** Cut every word not doing work. If a competitor's page could run the same headline, it's not specific enough.
- **No generic openers.** Ban "in today's fast-paced world," "are you tired of," "imagine a world where."

## 4. Outline the sections — one idea each

Before writing full paragraphs, list out the sections and assign each one job. If a section is doing two jobs, split it into two sections.

*Why fourth:* structure is cheap to fix in outline form and expensive to fix after you've written prose around it.

## 5. Draft each section: benefit before feature

For each section, write what the reader *gets* first, then — only if needed — the mechanism that delivers it. "Get paid in 2 days" before "automated payout scheduling."

*Why fifth:* leading with the feature is the single most common way B2B copy turns generic, because every competitor has a feature list. The benefit is what's actually specific to the buyer's situation.

- **1–3 bullets per section, max.** Needing more is a sign the section is trying to cover too much — go back to step 4 and split it.
- **Body copy supports the headline.** It doesn't repeat it or wander into new territory.

## 6. Handle the objection, right before the CTA

Take the objection(s) from step 2 and address them in one line each, placed just before the ask.

*Why sixth:* this only works once the rest of the page has already built the case — handling an objection too early feels defensive, and skipping it entirely leaves the reader with a reason to bounce right at the moment you're asking them to act.

## 7. Write the CTA: verb + outcome

Every button and link names the actual outcome, not a vague action.

- Weak: "Get Started," "Learn More," "Submit"
- Better: "Start your free trial," "See pricing," "Get my quote"

*Why seventh:* the CTA should be checkable against everything above it — if you can't name a specific outcome yet, the sections before it haven't earned it.

## 8. Fact-check every claim

Go through the full draft and check each claim.

- **No fabricated numbers or stats.** If you don't have it, don't invent it — flag it or cut it.
- **Back real claims with a number, name, or example.** "Fast" is a claim; "loads in under 400ms" is proof.

*Why eighth:* do this after the draft exists, not before — you need to see every claim you made in context to know which ones need backing and which should just be cut.

## 9. Edit pass: sentence-level robot tells

Now go sentence by sentence and strip the tells:

- **No em dashes.**
- **No two-beat antithesis** — cut "it's not just X, it's Y" and its variants.
- **No aphorism formulas** — cut anything that sounds like a poster caption.
- **Vary sentence length on purpose.** This is what actually fixes the "robot" sound — mix a 4-word sentence next to an 18-word one. Read it aloud; if it sounds like a narrator reading it, rewrite it.
- **Use contractions** where a person naturally would.

*Why ninth, not first:* these are surface fixes. Applying them to a structurally weak draft just makes a weak draft sound slightly more human — it doesn't make it convert. Fix strategy and structure first, then polish the sentences.

## 10. Cut for lazy readers

Final trim pass:

- No paragraph longer than ~3 lines on a rendered page.
- Cut anything that doesn't change whether the reader clicks or not.
- Front-load the point of every sentence and section.

*Why last:* you can only tell what's safe to cut once the page is complete — trimming too early risks cutting something you end up needing.

---

## Final self-check

Run through this before shipping. Anything that fails, go back to the relevant step above.

1. Could I delete this sentence and lose nothing?
2. Is every claim backed by a number, name, or example?
3. Could a competitor run this same headline?
4. Is there a hidden two-beat antithesis or aphorism?
5. Do the sentences vary in rhythm, or do they all sound the same?
6. Does the CTA name the actual outcome?
7. Did I handle the real objection before the CTA?
8. Am I talking to one buyer, or to "everyone"?
